The Harbour at Low Tide

The tide had gone out and taken the light with it. By four o'clock the harbour was a bowl of black mud, and the boats lay over on their sides like animals that had surrendered.

Máire stood at the end of the pier with the letter in her coat pocket and watched the gulls argue over something in the weed. She had read it three times on the train and once more on the walk down, and each time the same sentence turned over: we cannot keep the house after the spring.

Behind her the town was putting its lights on, one window at a time. Conn would be at the pub by now, or at the harbour office with the ledgers open and a glass he had forgotten to drink. She could go to either place. She could go home

The harbourmaster had left the gate unlocked again. She let herself through and stood where the water would be in six hours, on the flat dark ground, and listened to the boats settle.
