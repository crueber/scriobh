# How writers use planning boards: evidence report

Prepared 2026-11-21. Every source was accessed on that date unless noted. Provenance tags used throughout:

- OPENED: the page or PDF text was read in full or in the relevant part.
- QUOTED: the writer's words as quoted on a page that was read; the original was not read.
- SNIPPET: seen only as search-result text. Do not rely on these without checking.
- SELF-SELECTED: the respondents chose to answer; the result describes respondents, not writers in general.

---

## 1. Executive summary

**The honest headline: no survey anywhere measures what share of writers use boards, cards, sticky notes, whiteboards or a corkboard view, in any population, professional or amateur.** I searched surveys by Reedsy, ALLi, Writer's Digest, the Authors Guild, Written Word Media, Draft2Digital, NaNoWriMo and its survey partners, Literature & Latte, Plottr, Dabble, Campfire, WGA, Final Draft, ScreenCraft, Stephen Follows and John August, plus academic work. None asked the question, or none published an answer. Any number that circulates for "percent of writers who use index cards" has no source behind it that I could find. Confidence that the gap is real: high (many routes tried; the one vendor with the biggest sample, Literature & Latte, surveyed about 24,000 users in 2020 and never published feature-level results).

What the evidence does support:

1. **Planning before drafting is common, and a board is one of several ways to do it.** Among 22 creative writers in a 2025 CHI-paper questionnaire, about 87% made a "tangible plan" before the first draft; the most common form was bullet-point notes, written on a whiteboard, notebook, note app or word processor. Sticky notes were mentioned, with no percentage. Confidence: low (n=22, mostly under 35, Canadian-heavy).
2. **Plotter versus pantser polls cluster around "a bit under half plan in detail."** Self-selected polls: 40% plotters in a forced-choice Facebook poll (n=87, 2019); 46% "detailed plotter" or "I do an outline" in a Twitter poll (n=167, 2022); 48% "yes, I use an outline" in a 2010 blog poll (n=109); 36% plotters in a 2020 poll of unstated size. A workshop leader's show of hands among published writers gave "about half." Confidence in "roughly 35 to 50% plan with an outline": low to medium. None of these asks about boards.
3. **Seasoned professionals in film and TV use boards heavily, by their own accounts.** The Breaking Bad and Better Call Saul rooms used 3x5 cards on cork boards (named writers quoted); TV writer Kate Powers says she has "pretty much only worked in rooms where we favored 3-by-5 index cards on 3-by-4 cork boards" while "many shows prefer white boards"; Jane Espenson compares cards and whiteboards directly. Screenwriting craft teaching (Syd Field, Blake Snyder, John August, Alexandra Sokoloff) treats cards as standard. These are accounts, not counts. Confidence that boards are mainstream in TV and film breaking: medium to high. Confidence in any percentage: none available.
4. **Professionals in prose fiction are split, and the split is documented by named examples, not data.** Card or grid users: Nabokov, Crichton (second-hand), Rowling (a hand-drawn grid), Jennifer Crusie, Chuck Wendig, Julie Cohen, Laura Drake (spreadsheet), John McPhee (nonfiction). Avoiders of outlines: Stephen King, George R.R. Martin, Elmore Leonard, Diana Gabaldon, Lee Child, Terry Pratchett. Several of the avoiders are stated from search snippets and need a primary check (section 3).
5. **Digital versus physical: no data on the split.** The one controlled study found (Jensen et al., CSCW 2018, 14 designers, not writers) saw no difference in ideation output; physical suited creating and posting notes, digital invited more interaction with notes. Writers' accounts describe hybrids: physical for "macro mapping," digital for storage. Vendor claims put the digital planning-tool user base in the tens of thousands per product (Plottr 40,000+, Dabble 21,000+), which is small beside the 24,000 Scrivener survey responses alone.
6. **The most reliable tool-share number for published authors:** 12% named Scrivener, which has a corkboard, as the tool they use most (Bernoff survey, 2019 to 2022, 172 published nonfiction authors); Word was 61%. This says nothing about whether Scrivener users open the corkboard.

It speaks to planning, not to boards.

## 4. How boards are used (design-relevant)

Strength labels are on each line; nearly all of this is craft advice and individual practice.

**What goes on a card**
- One scene or sequence per card, in a few words. August (via the Final Draft summary): maximum seven words; "a story point, be it a scene or a sequence"; "Keep cards general enough that they can be rearranged" ("Battle in swamp", not "Final showdown"). Sokoloff: one or two lines. McPhee: one code word.
- Film is said to run to 40 to 60 cards; August: "Most movies can be summarized in less than 50 cards"; Snyder's board is 40. Sokoloff keeps 40 to 60 cards for a novel by merging scenes into chapters.
- Cards are not only scenes. Final Draft's own guidance lists A, B and C stories, a character, a location, images. Writers also keep mood boards, timelines and mind maps alongside (CHI 2025 questionnaire).
- Story Genius uses a longer card (what happens, why it matters, what the character realises). SNIPPET only.
- Scrivener's card has a title and a synopsis area (vendor manual).

**Colour**
- By sequence type: August, action purple and narrative blue, to show pacing.
- By story strand: A, B and C stories in TV; Final Draft beat board colours.
- By POV: Drake's spreadsheet colours POV, plus romance beats, cuttable scenes and problem scenes.
- By character: August's letter code on cards "helpful for figuring out who's missing."
- By status: Scrivener's diagonal status stamp. No source found on how writers on physical boards mark status.
- By label for timelines and POV: Scrivener's own blog suggests labels to track "multiple timelines and point-of-view characters."
- No source ranks which colour dimension writers choose most. Sokoloff finds subplot colours distracting. Colour is optional in every account.

**Rows and columns**
- Save the Cat's board: 40 cards. The four-row layout (Act 1, Act 2a, Act 2b, Act 3) is in secondary guides only.
- Sokoloff: four vertical columns of 10 to 15 cards, or eight columns of 5 to 8, with anchor cards (Act One Climax, Midpoint Climax, Act Two Climax, Climax).
- TV board: episodes across the top, characters down the side (Hartman); act structure (teaser plus six acts) laid out per episode (Engler).
- Rowling's grid: one row per chapter implied, columns for chapter number, month, title, outline, then one column per subplot.
- Story Grid spreadsheet: one row per scene; column groups for identity, change, continuity (POV, period, duration, location), avatars, and the five commandments. Framed as a diagnosis tool for finished work.
- Drake: one row per chapter; far-right timeline column.
- Scrivener corkboard: cards flow left to right then in rows, mirroring the binder; a freeform mode lets you place cards anywhere, then commit the order.
- August says a flat table "often works better than a vertical (a corkboard)."

**When in the process**
- Pre-draft: McPhee (after research, before drafting), Crichton, Snyder (including his "Rubik's Cube" shuffle, offered for when the outline feels too mechanical: "you never know what insights will be realized"), Breaking Bad ("we put everything up on the board before the beat by beat process of breaking story began").
- During drafting: Nabokov composed on cards. Rowling's grid timing is unknown.
- Revision: Sokoloff (put the existing draft on cards to find weak spots); Cohen (the Post-it plan becomes the revision road map); Story Grid (diagnose the existing work). A vendor says its beat board is for before writing and its card view for refining after. SNIPPET.
- Some writers use boards only at the start: Heisserer consolidates the cards into one document.

**Multiple boards**
- Scrivener: each folder has its own corkboard; a stacked view shows several. Writers' Room Pro lists separate episodic, character and scrap boards (help-centre titles only). Hartman: "Whiteboards all over the place." Sokoloff keeps a practice grid for analysing a film beside the working one. No source documents how many boards a typical project has.

**Moving cards**
- The act every account stresses: Snyder (shuffle as a way to break out of a mechanical outline), Sokoloff ("moved, discarded, or duplicated to test scenes in different positions"), Espenson ("effortlessly"), McPhee (move until a structure appears), Murch (cards can be rearranged when structure changes), August ("Cards are cheap. Don't hesitate to rework them").
- A Scrivener forum post (SNIPPET): a user wants software that retains each note "as its own unit, and to lay them out in front of me for organization."
- Cost of the physical form: a moved board "can throw the work in disarray," and there is "no support for remote collaboration on the same board" (Jensen et al., paraphrasing the literature). Rooms photograph boards; Roiland's remark on virtual boards is the one verified statement.
- Why moving matters, in theory: Kirsh's work on epistemic actions argues that rearranging objects reduces mental effort. That is a framework; I found no study of writers moving cards.

**Collaboration**
- TV rooms are collaborative by definition; the board is a shared surface. The 2020 shift to Zoom produced Miro boards, Writers Room Pro (which "emulates the white board and index cards normally used in a production office"), shared Google Docs and the writers' assistant sharing a screen. No data on how many rooms stayed virtual afterwards.

## 5. Digital versus physical

- **No survey data on the split exists in anything I found.**
- Accounts of physical: Crusie ("Words on paper feel like story"), McPhee, Caro, Murch (kept a physical board), Doug Nelson on Stage32 (big paper, cue cards: digital tools "can't replace that for 'macro mapping'"). Break rooms in TV for Breaking Bad.
- Accounts of digital: Scrivener users, Final Draft beat board, Miro and Writers Room Pro in the 2020 rooms; Janine Latus replaced index cards and thumb tacks with Scrivener (vendor testimonial).
- Accounts of hybrid: Heisserer (cards, then one document), CHI 2025 respondents (whiteboard, notebook, spreadsheet, app, with "many scattered documents"), Cohen (Post-its).
- Writers' own limits on digital: a CHI 2025 respondent felt "confined to viewing [their text] in a list format"; others keep "peripheral reminders" on physical whiteboards.
- Trend over time: no longitudinal data found. Weak signals only: Scrivener launched 2007 and Final Draft added a Beat Board; the 2004 August survey listed index cards among features "no one uses" in screenwriting software, while 2000s craft writing still treated physical cards as standard. Product pages show many card or grid tools (Plottr, Dabble, Novelcrafter, Campfire, Milanote), which shows supply, not use. Google Trends was not attempted.
- Scale: the largest user-count evidence is vendor-supplied (Plottr 40,000+, Dabble 21,000+, Novelcrafter 252k+ "community," Scrivener over 24,000 survey responses). Literature & Latte declined to give a user count in January 2021.
- Professional versus hobbyist: the only split in any data is Follows 2019 (Final Draft 93% of professionals have used it versus 76% of aspiring, for software brands only). The CHI 2025 sample is mostly amateurs under 35.

## 6. Gaps and caveats

1. **No prevalence number for board use.** Not for any writer population. No survey asks about boards, corkboards, index cards, sticky notes, whiteboards or wall plotting, and none separates professional from amateur on those practices.
2. **Plotter versus pantser polls measure something else.** They ask about advance planning, not boards, and they are self-selected, small, and use mixed category sets. The 64/36, 40/60, and 46/54 splits are not comparable to each other. A "plotter" does not necessarily use a board; a pantser may use one mid-draft.
3. **Professional evidence is accounts, not data.** Survivorship and visibility bias: writers who use striking visual methods (a wall of cards, a Rowling grid) get photographed and quoted. Avoiders are less visible.
4. **Medium matters.** TV writers' rooms are a different population from solo novelists. Rooms break stories collectively on a board because the board is the shared surface; a novelist alone has less reason. Do not generalise a TV room practice to novelists.
5. **Vendor claims.** Almost every figure on tool use and almost every statement about card colour or process in Scrivener, Final Draft, Plottr and similar products is from a company selling them.
6. **Second-hand quotes.** Several well-known claims (Crichton's table, Nabokov's quote, Rowling's grid timing, Stephen King, Martin, Leonard) rest on secondary pages or snippets. The Paris Review pages would not load for me.
7. **Misattributions to watch for.** The "about half of published writers are plotters" line appears on Jane Friedman's blog but was written by guest author Jess Lourey. The August index-card hints I read are Ken Miyamoto's summary of an August post, not August's own words. The 2004 August survey mention of index cards refers to a software feature, not to physical cards.
8. **The research helper's tool limits.** SearXNG returned empty late in the session; some pages (ACM, Cloudflare-protected university repositories, Reddit, Paris Review) could not be read. I did not try to bypass these.
9. **Not tested:** Google Trends, Reddit polls (blocked), Facebook group polls, Goodreads polls with real sample sizes, the Authors Guild's own members' tool data, ALLi's full survey PDFs.

**Best proxies for what the owner wants to know:**
- Share of writers who plan before drafting: the 2019 to 2022 polls (36% to 48% detailed or outline) plus the 87% in the small CHI questionnaire. Treat as "somewhere between a third and most."
- Share who use a board: no proxy. The best structural proxy is Scrivener, a corkboard product, being the primary tool for 12% of published nonfiction authors and the focus of 24,000 survey responses; that sets an order of magnitude for a digital corkboard user base, with corkboard use inside it unknown.
- Seasoned professionals: the strongest indicator is first-hand "most rooms I worked in" statements from TV writers (Powers, Hutchinson) and the consistent craft teaching in film, with a clear split in prose fiction.

## 7. Implications for a writing app's board or corkboard view

Brief, and each follows from a documented practice, not from prevalence.

1. **Moving cards is the main act; keep it cheap and reversible.** Every account stresses reordering and trying alternatives. Moving should be one gesture, with undo, and a way to try an order without committing.
2. **A card is a title plus a short synopsis, with a short default length.** The practitioners keep cards to a handful of words and general enough to move. The app should show the title large and the synopsis small, and let a card be a scene or a chapter.
3. **Colour is optional and has several meanings.** POV, plotline or strand, status, character, and sequence type each appear. Offer more than one coded dimension, none forced, and keep it readable with colour off (letter codes).
4. **Support a grid as well as a free layout.** Acts as columns (Sokoloff, Snyder), episodes by character (TV), chapters by subplot (Rowling), scenes by continuity columns (Story Grid, Drake). One surface that can be sorted into rows and columns covers these.
5. **Offer it at more than one stage.** Pre-draft planning, mid-draft re-sorting, and revision diagnosis (put the existing draft on cards) are all documented. Making cards from an existing draft is the revision case.
6. **Allow several boards per project, and a stacked view.** Folder-level boards, a character board, a scrap board, a practice board.
7. **Show the whole at a glance and keep a way to read the whole at once.** Espenson's drawback with cards is losing the whole-episode view; August and Murch both lean on laying everything out flat. A one-screen overview matters at scale.
8. **Expect hybrids.** Physical for macro mapping and photographed, digital for storage and moving. A photograph import or a print-ready board export fits that habit better than a claim to replace paper.
9. **Do not assume the audience.** Professionals split. Some of the best-documented avoiders (King, Martin, Leonard, Child, Gabaldon) would never open a board. The board should be an optional lens on the same manuscript, not the way in.

Ranked by strength of evidence: items 1, 2 and 4 are well supported by several named practitioners; 3 and 6 are supported but thin; 7 and 8 rest on a few accounts; 5 is craft-book advice.
